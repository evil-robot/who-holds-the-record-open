# Evidence: what it costs when the record does not travel

**Purpose.** Sources for the "travelling patient" feature of Who Holds the Record: a person moves between countries (within Europe, or the US to Europe and back) and their health record does not follow. Each entry gives the citation, link, the exact figure and its scope, the design and quality, how we read it, and one line on what it supports and what it does not.

**Compiled** 2 October 2026. Every source below was opened. "Full text" means the document itself was read; "abstract" means the abstract was read through Europe PMC and the full text was not. Live dashboards and Eurostat tables carry the date we pulled them.

**The headline caution.** There is almost no direct, peer-reviewed measurement of harm or cost caused specifically by a record failing to cross a national border. What exists is (a) EU projections of savings from cross-border exchange, (b) official counts of how many people get care abroad, (c) within-country studies, mostly from the US, of what happens when clinicians lack outside information or gain it through exchange. The feature can honestly combine (b) with (a) and (c) only if it says that (c) is borrowed from domestic settings.

## 1. Costs and harms when records do not travel

### 1.1 Cross-border specific

**1.1.1 Cross-border prescriptions not dispensed (EU).**
European Commission (2022). *Impact assessment report accompanying the proposal for a Regulation on the European Health Data Space.* SWD(2022) 131 final, Part 1/4, Strasbourg, 3 May 2022. https://health.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2022-05/ehealth_ehds_2022ia_1_en_0.pdf (full text).
- Figure: "almost 8 million cross-border prescriptions are presented for dispensation per year in EU, with a non-dispensation rate of 46%, which could generate up to EUR 240 million in unnecessary costs yearly" (p. 25). Elsewhere: "around 7.8 million", "down from 55% in 2012" (p. 50). Foreign prescriptions per pharmacy per month rose from 1.46 (2012) to 5.87 (2021) (p. 20).
- Scope: EU-wide, per year, "preliminary results" from the Directive 2011/24 evaluation. The 46% rests on "a low response rate of 158 pharmacists across 5 countries" (footnote cxli). The EUR 240 million assumes each non-dispensed prescription leads to a GP visit at EUR 65.77, a population-weighted Eurostat average (footnote cxlii).
- Quality: low. Small, self-selected pharmacist survey; cost is an assumption-driven extrapolation. The impact assessment gives no independent source for the 7.8 million volume either: it cites the same Directive evaluation, and the figure is consistent with scaling the surveyed 5.87 foreign prescriptions per pharmacy per month to all EU pharmacies. Treat volume and rate as resting on the same small sample. The evaluation itself (SWD(2022) 200, below) lists pharmacist responses of 72 (PL), 55 (FR), 26 (NL), 4 (DE) and 1 more, against 250 invited, and notes low response rates.
- Supports: cross-border prescriptions are common and a large share fail at the pharmacy counter, for reasons the Commission ties to verification, language and missing information. Does not support: a precise EU cost, any per-country cost, or any harm to patients.

**1.1.2 Continuity of care after treatment abroad (EU public consultation).**
European Commission (2022). *Commission Staff Working Document accompanying the Report on the operation of Directive 2011/24/EU.* SWD(2022) 200 final, Brussels, 12 May 2022. Retrieved from the Publications Office: http://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/52022SC0200 (full text).
- Figure: problems with continuity of care after treatment abroad were reported by 84 of 181 public consultation respondents (46%), "mainly concern[ing] unrecognised medical prescriptions/referrals, lack of effective transfer of medical records between treating doctors and difficulties in accessing follow-up treatment". 46% said providers transferred records or a patient summary home "to a great or to some extent"; 41% said "to a limited extent or not at all" (p. 25 to 26). 38% knew of pharmacists abroad not recognising prescriptions (p. 26). The evaluation concludes the Directive "did not completely resolve the issues (language, verification and authenticity problems)" with prescriptions.
- Scope: open public consultation, 181 respondents (patients, citizens, professionals, organisations), 2021.
- Quality: low for prevalence (self-selected respondents), good as an official record of the barrier.
- Supports: the Commission's own evaluation names failed record transfer as a recurring problem for cross-border patients. Does not support: any rate of record failure among patients in general.

**1.1.3 Medical tourism complications that land on the home system (UK, US).**
- Hanefeld J, Horsfall D, Lunt N, Smith R (2013). Medical tourism: a cost or benefit to the NHS? *PLoS ONE* 8(10):e70406. doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0070406 (full text). Funded by NIHR HS&DR (09/2001/21). Figures: about 63,000 UK residents travelled abroad for treatment in 2010 and about 52,000 came to the UK (International Passenger Survey); identified NHS costs of complications of GBP 15.5 million a year (fertility tourism) and GBP 8.2 million a year (cosmetic surgery). Quality: moderate; mixed methods, cost figures built from specific case sources. Supports: outward medical travel creates follow-up costs for the home system. Does not support: that missing records cause those costs (the paper does not isolate records).
- England C, et al. (2025). Complications and costs to the NHS due to outward medical tourism for elective surgery: a rapid review. *medRxiv* preprint. doi:10.1101/2025.04.02.25325086 (abstract). **Preprint, not peer reviewed.** 37 studies (35 case series or reports), 655 patients treated by the NHS 2006 to 2024 for complications of surgery abroad, 61% after surgery in Turkey. Supports: complications after treatment abroad are documented. Does not support: a rate or national cost (case series only).
- Stoney RJ, Kozarsky P, Walker AT, Gaines J (2022). Population-based surveillance of medical tourism among U.S. residents from 11 states and territories: findings from the BRFSS. *Infect Control Hosp Epidemiol* 43(7):870-875. doi:10.1017/ice.2021.245 (full text, PMC8922991). 517 of 93,492 respondents (0.55%) travelled abroad for planned care in 2016; extrapolated to about 1.13 million US residents (95% CI 0.90 to 1.41 million); 5% reported complications abroad; 67% sought follow-up care in the US. Quality: moderate; population survey in 11 jurisdictions, single year. Supports: two-thirds of US medical travellers come home needing a US clinician to pick up care given abroad. Does not support: anything about records directly.

**1.1.4 Tourists and repatriated patients in emergency care (Europe).**
- Turcato G, et al. (2023). Impact of tourists on emergency department in a high-tourism alpine area. *Travel Med Infect Dis* 51:102491. doi:10.1016/j.tmaid.2022.102491 (abstract). Merano, Italy, 2017 to 2019: 19,469 of 208,875 ED presentations (9.3%) were tourists; tourists had a higher share of severe triage codes. Single site. Supports: in tourist regions, a meaningful share of emergency patients are people whose records sit in another system. Does not support: outcomes of missing records.
- Brockhus L, et al. (2021). Repatriations of ill and injured travelers and emigrants to Switzerland, 2013 to 2018. *Int J Environ Res Public Health* 18(5):2777. doi:10.3390/ijerph18052777 (abstract). 447 patients repatriated to Bern; 79% from European countries; median cost CHF 12,006 per case. Single site. Supports: a patient treated abroad comes home into a second hospital episode. Does not support: duplication or harm attributable to records.

### 1.2 Within-country evidence on missing information (borrowed, mostly US and Canada)

**1.2.1 Missing clinical information in primary care.**
Smith PC, Araya-Guerra R, Bublitz C, et al. (2005). Missing clinical information during primary care visits. *JAMA* 293(5):565-571. doi:10.1001/jama.293.5.565 (abstract). AHRQ funded.
- Figure: clinicians reported missing information in 13.6% of 1,614 visits (32 Colorado clinics, 2003). Missing information was judged at least somewhat likely to harm the patient in 44% of those cases and to delay care or add services in 59.5%. Missing information was more likely for **recent immigrants (OR 1.78, 95% CI 1.06 to 2.99)** and new patients (OR 2.39), and less likely where clinicians had full electronic records (OR 0.40).
- Quality: moderate; cross-sectional, clinician-reported, harm is perceived not measured.
- Supports: the one peer-reviewed link we found between having moved across a border and the record being missing. Does not support: measured harm, or any European rate.

**1.2.2 Information gaps in the emergency department.**
Stiell A, Forster AJ, Stiell IG, van Walraven C (2003). Prevalence of information gaps in the emergency department and the effect on patient outcomes. *CMAJ* 169(10):1023-1028. PMID 14609971 (abstract).
- Figure: at least one gap in 32.2% of 1,002 visits (95% CI 29.4 to 35.2); most often medical history (58%); judged essential to care in 47.8%; adjusted ED stay 1.2 hours longer when a gap was present; no association with admission.
- Quality: moderate; single Canadian teaching hospital, prospective.
- Supports: "emergency care without history" is common and slows care even within one country. Does not support: mortality or cost.

**1.2.3 Medication history errors at admission.**
Tam VC, Knowles SR, Cornish PL, et al. (2005). Frequency, type and clinical importance of medication history errors at admission to hospital: a systematic review. *CMAJ* 173(5):510-515. doi:10.1503/cmaj.045311 (abstract).
- Figure: 22 studies, 3,755 patients. Errors in prescription medication histories in up to 67% of cases; in the 5 studies that separated intentional changes, 27% to 54% of patients had at least one error; 11% to 59% of errors were clinically important (6 studies).
- Quality: systematic review of observational studies; wide ranges.
- Supports: when the clinician cannot see the medication list, the history taken is often wrong. Does not support: a cross-border rate.

**1.2.4 Medication discrepancies at transitions (WHO).**
World Health Organization (2019). *Medication safety in transitions of care.* WHO/UHC/SDS/2019.9, 20 June 2019. https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/ea908758-6760-4309-b884-bb55215c71da/content (full text). CC BY-NC-SA 3.0 IGO.
- Figure: 3.4% to 97% of adult and 22% to 72.3% of paediatric patients had at least one medication discrepancy on admission (p. 15); "The global cost of medication errors has been estimated at US$ 42 billion annually" (p. 18).
- Caveat: the US$42 billion traces to Aitken and Gorokhovich (2012), IMS Institute for Healthcare Informatics, an industry analytics firm. It covers all medication errors worldwide, not transitions and not borders.
- Supports: transitions of care are a recognised global medication-safety risk. Does not support: a cost of cross-border record gaps. Do not put US$42 billion next to a travelling patient.

**1.2.5 Discharge information transfer.**
Kripalani S, LeFevre F, Phillips CO, et al. (2007). Deficits in communication and information transfer between hospital-based and primary care physicians. *JAMA* 297(8):831-841. doi:10.1001/jama.297.8.831 (abstract).
- Figure: discharge summary available at the first post-discharge visit in 12% to 34% of cases, 51% to 77% at four weeks, affecting care in about 25% of follow-up visits; test results missing from 33% to 63% of summaries.
- Quality: systematic review (55 observational, 18 intervention studies).
- Supports: records fail to follow patients even between two providers in one country. Does not support: cross-border rates.

**1.2.6 Repeat imaging after transfer between hospitals.**
- Summers LN, Harry ML, Colling KP (2021). Evaluating our progress with trauma transfer imaging. *Emerg Radiol* 28(5):939-948. doi:10.1007/s10140-021-01938-x (abstract). One US trauma centre, 2018 to 2019: of 68 transferred patients with CT at the first hospital, 19% had a repeat CT of the same region; 76% went back to the scanner.
- Vernon SA, et al. (2019). CT in trauma patients accepted in transfer. *Kans J Med* 12(1):7-10. doi:10.17161/kjm.v12i1.11697 (abstract). Repeat CT in 47.2% of transfers; "preventable" reasons (refusal to read outside films, incompatible software, preference) fell from 25.8% to 14.3% after software and education changes.
- Quality: low (single-site retrospective). Supports: images that do not travel get repeated, with extra radiation. Does not support: a population rate.

## 2. The EHDS impact assessment and MyHealth@EU use

**2.1 EHDS impact assessment (the primary source).**
European Commission (2022). SWD(2022) 131 final, Part 1/4 (link in 1.1.1; full text).
- Primary use (patients and care), preferred Policy Option 2: "EUR 5.4 billion savings for patients and healthcare providers over the course of 10 years, including those stemming from a greater uptake of telemedicine and cross-border interoperability of ePrescriptions and medical images" (p. 54).
- Secondary use (research, policy, regulation), Option 2: "at least EUR 5.4 billion over the next 10 years" (p. 55).
- Cross-border alone, baseline: MyHealth@EU "could result in EUR 1.9-2.8 billion in savings through the services of electronic cross-border prescriptions (corresponding to EUR 37-52 million additional dispensations over 10 years) and could save additional EUR 19-75 million through the exchange of medical images alone" (p. 50). The source text says "EUR 37-52 million additional dispensations"; read it as 37 to 52 million dispensations. Lower bound assumes 10% yearly growth in cross-border prescriptions, upper 20%.
- Cross-border alone, extra from mandatory faster rollout (Options 2 and 3): "additional EUR 173-232 million in cost savings in the area of ePrescriptions and medical imaging alone" over 10 years, against MyHealth@EU costs of EUR 39 to 109 million (p. 54).
- Within-country imaging (context only): "Ensuring interoperability at national level could contribute to reduce duplicated medical imaging, which is estimated at EUR 14 billion in the EU over ten years", calculated as an assumed 10% duplication on EUR 14 billion a year of CT, MRI and PET (Eurostat) (p. 50, footnote clxx). The 10% is an assumption, not a measurement, and this is national interoperability, not cross-border.
- Baseline context: patient summaries and ePrescriptions existed in two-thirds of Member States; "10 out of 27 Member States allow their patients to share their patient summaries and ePrescriptions" via MyHealth@EU (p. 20); "over 200 million Europeans have a European Health Insurance Card" (p. 20).
- Quality: a regulator's ex ante projection. It models, it does not observe. Several inputs are assumptions or small surveys (see 1.1.1).
- Supports: the Commission projected about EUR 5.4 billion of care-side savings EU-wide over 10 years from the preferred option, of which the cross-border ePrescription and imaging part is roughly EUR 1.9 to 2.8 billion plus EUR 0.17 to 0.23 billion. Does not support: realised savings, per-country savings, per-patient savings, or savings under the adopted Regulation (EU) 2025/327, which differs from the 2022 proposal.

**2.2 The public "EUR 11 billion".**
European Commission (2024). *Questions and Answers on the European Health Data Space.* QANDA/24/2251, 24 April 2024. https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/qanda_24_2251 (full text via the press corner API).
- Figure: "Overall, the EHDS it is expected to save the EU around €11 billion over ten years: €5.5 billion will be saved from better access and exchange of health data in healthcare and another €5.4 billion will be saved from better use of health data for research, innovation and policy making." Also: "Today, only eleven Member States support the sharing of patient summaries and ePrescriptions via MyHealth@EU."
- Note: the impact assessment says EUR 5.4 billion for primary use; the Q&A says EUR 5.5 billion. Cite the impact assessment for the number and the Q&A only for the rounded public total.
- Supports: "The Commission projects about EUR 11 billion of savings across the EU over ten years, roughly half from care and half from research use." Does not support: "the record gap costs Europe EUR 11 billion", or any cross-border-only figure (cross-border is a small slice, see 2.1).

**2.3 MyHealth@EU actual use (live dashboard, under revision).**
European Commission, DG SANTE. *MyHealth@EU KPI Monitoring Framework (V2).* https://webgate.ec.europa.eu/santegis/eHDSI/ ; data files at `.../eHDSI/data/kpi-<id>.json`. Pulled 2 October 2026; latest quarter in the data 2026-Q2. The dashboard carries this banner: "the MyHealth@EU Monitoring Framework KPIs are currently undergoing revision for the purpose of increasing the accuracy of the data on cross-border transactions".
- Each transaction is reported by both countries, so summing all rows double counts. We counted only rows reported by the sending country (field `country` equal to `from`) and removed test transactions:

| Year | ePrescriptions exchanged (KPI 1.3) | eDispensations (KPI 1.4) | Patient Summaries (KPI 1.5) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2022 | 21,836 | 8,438 | 116 |
| 2023 | 29,224 | 11,590 | 258 |
| 2024 | 45,030 | 15,818 | 1,186 |

| 2026 H1 | 23,429 | 6,153 | 94 |

- Reading the receiving-country rows instead gives similar totals (2025: 47,687 ePrescriptions, 18,012 eDispensations, 634 Patient Summaries). Use "about 50,000 ePrescriptions and about 18,000 dispensations abroad in 2025".
- The largest 2025 flows were Estonia to Finland (17,063 ePrescriptions) and Finland to Estonia (10,515). Unique citizens using the Patient Summary service (KPI 1.12): 1,034 in 2025.
- Countries live (KPI 1.1), 2026-Q2: 17, that is 16 EU Member States plus Norway (CY, CZ, EE, EL, ES, FI, FR, HR, IE, LT, LU, LV, MT, NL, NO, PL, PT).
- **Do not use** KPI 1.11 for 2025 (1,167,427 unique ePrescription citizens against 10,189 in 2024 and 7,769 in 2026 H1). It is inconsistent with the years on either side and the dashboard is under revision; treat it as a reporting error, not growth.
- Supports: against roughly 8 million paper or foreign prescriptions presented a year (1.1.1), electronic cross-border exchange is in the tens of thousands, and patient summaries in the hundreds. Does not support: precise counts (the data are under revision).

**2.4 MyHealth@EU, peer-reviewed snapshot.**
Bruthans J, Jiráková K (2023). The current state and usage of European electronic cross-border health services (eHDSI). *J Med Syst* 47(1):21. doi:10.1007/s10916-023-01920-9 (full text, PMC9918835).
- Figure: DG SANTE data through Q2 2022. Estonia sent 2,673 to 7,423 ePrescriptions a quarter and Finland up to 3,316; patient summaries exchanged per country per quarter were mostly under 100. Authors: "Only eP/eD service is commonly and widely used so far and only Estonian and Finnish patients usually get their medication dispensed abroad."
- Supports: a citable, peer-reviewed statement that cross-border record exchange was close to zero outside one country pair. Does not support: current figures.

## 3. Savings from interoperable records and health information exchange (HIE)

Almost all of this evidence is from the US and within one country. The EHDS impact assessment extrapolates from literature of this kind. Present the reviews as a set, because they disagree in tone.

**3.1 Systematic reviews (read together).**
- Rahurkar S, Vest JR, Menachemi N (2015). Despite the spread of health information exchange, there is little evidence of its impact on cost, use, and quality of care. *Health Aff* 34(3):477-483. doi:10.1377/hlthaff.2014.0729 (abstract). 27 studies; 57% of analyses reported some benefit, but strong designs were less likely to: of six, one negative, three null, two positive. "Little generalizable evidence currently exists."
- Hersh WR, Totten AM, Eden KB, et al. (2015). Outcomes from health information exchange: systematic review and future research needs. *JMIR Med Inform* 3(4):e39. doi:10.2196/medinform.5215 (abstract; AHRQ review, PROSPERO CRD42014013285). 34 studies. "Low-quality evidence generally finds that HIE reduces duplicative laboratory and radiology testing, emergency department costs, hospital admissions." No study reported clinical outcomes or harms.
- Menachemi N, Rahurkar S, Harle CA, Vest JR (2018). The benefits of health information exchange: an updated systematic review. *JAMIA* 25(9):1259-1265. doi:10.1093/jamia/ocy035 (abstract). 24 studies, 63 analyses, mostly US (about 40% from New York HIEs). All 7 causal-design studies reported some benefit (fewer duplicated procedures, less imaging, lower costs, better safety); none reported harm.
- Supports: by 2018 the better-designed US studies point to fewer repeat tests and images when outside records are available. Does not support: a single effect size, clinical outcomes, or transfer to European cross-border settings.

**3.2 Primary studies with effect sizes.**
| Study | Setting, design | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Lammers EJ, Adler-Milstein J, Kocher KE (2014). *Med Care* 52(3):227-234. doi:10.1097/MLR.0000000000000067 (abstract) | California and Florida EDs, 2007 to 2010; 37 EDs joining HIE vs 410 not; fixed effects | Repeat imaging at unaffiliated EDs within 30 days fell by 8.7 points for CT (95% CI -14.7 to -2.7), 9.1 for ultrasound, 13.0 for chest x-ray, a 44% to 67% relative reduction. Baseline repeat rates 14.7% (CT), 20.7% (US), 19.5% (x-ray). |
| Vest JR, Kaushal R, Silver MD, et al. (2014). *Am J Manag Care* 20(11 Spec No. 17):eSP16-24. PMID 25811815 (abstract) | 196,314 consenting patients, New York community HIE, 2009 to 2010; cohort | 7.7% of images repeated within 90 days; 5% with HIE access vs 8% without. |

**3.3 The counter-finding (include it).**
McCormick D, Bor DH, Woolhandler S, Himmelstein DU (2012). Giving office-based physicians electronic access to patients' prior imaging and lab results did not deter ordering of tests. *Health Aff* 31(3):488-496. doi:10.1377/hlthaff.2011.0876 (abstract). 28,741 visits, 1,187 US office physicians, 2008: electronic access to imaging results was associated with a 40% to 70% higher likelihood of ordering imaging. Cross-sectional. Supports: access alone does not guarantee fewer tests. A fair page cites this beside 3.1.

**3.4 Modelled national savings (weak; context only).**
- Walker J, Pan E, Johnston D, et al. (2005). The value of health care information exchange and interoperability. *Health Aff* Suppl Web Exclusives W5-10. doi:10.1377/hlthaff.w5.10 (abstract). Model "combined published evidence with expert opinion": USD 77.8 billion a year net once fully standardised (US). Funding not verified here.
- Hillestad R, Bigelow J, Bower A, et al. (2005). Can electronic medical record systems transform health care? *Health Aff* 24(5):1103-1117. doi:10.1377/hlthaff.24.5.1103 (abstract). "More than USD 81 billion annually" (US). **Industry funded**: RAND states funding came from Cerner, General Electric, Hewlett-Packard, Johnson & Johnson and Xerox (https://www.rand.org/news/press/2005/09/14.html). RAND's own later review found the promised savings had not appeared (Kellermann AL, Jones SS (2013). *Health Aff* 32(1):63-68. doi:10.1377/hlthaff.2012.0693, abstract).
- OECD (2017). *Tackling Wasteful Spending on Health.* OECD Publishing, Paris. doi:10.1787/9789264266414-en (full text). "Existing estimates suggest that one-fifth of health spending could be channelled towards better use"; wasteful care explicitly includes "duplicated interventions, such as diagnostic tests, that are repeated due to inadequate information exchange or care co-ordination between providers" (Box 2.1). No figure isolates duplication from record gaps. Supports the category, not a number.

## 4. How many people get care outside their home country

**4.1 Care under the EU social security rules (the largest official channel).**
De Wispelaere F, De Smedt L, Pacolet J (2026). *Cross-border healthcare in the EU under social security coordination, reference year 2024.* European Commission, DG EMPL. doi:10.2767/8935245 (full text). Published 13 March 2026.
- About 256 million EHICs in circulation in 2024, about 51% of EU/EFTA/UK citizens.
- Unplanned necessary care during a temporary stay (EHIC): about EUR 1.1 billion in 2024, 0.1% of spending on benefits in kind. The report gives claim counts by country but no EU total. Summing the 2024 E125 claims received in its Table 5 gives about 1.78 million across 28 reporting rows, **incomplete** (no 2024 row for Italy, Cyprus, Luxembourg, Norway; Belgium is 2021 data). Claims are not persons.
- Planned care (PD S2): more than 60,000 forms issued, fewer than 10 per 100,000 insured; under EUR 200 million (excluding Luxembourg).
- Living in one country, insured in another (PD S1): about 2.3 million people, 0.5% of insured persons; about EUR 3.2 billion.
- Supports: hundreds of millions carry the right to care abroad, and the bill for unplanned care abroad alone is about EUR 1.1 billion a year. Does not support: a count of patients (claims and forms are not people) or any cost of missing records.

**4.2 Care under Directive 2011/24 (the smaller channel).**
- European Commission (2026). *Member State data on cross-border patient healthcare following Directive 2011/24/EU, reference year 2024.* Published 12 May 2026. https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b92c70f2-7415-465d-82c9-508f595fe880_en?filename=crossborder_2024_patient-healthcare_data_en.pdf (full text). 2024: 6,350 prior authorisation requests (20 countries), 74.7% authorised, EUR 8.86 million reimbursed (10 countries); 292,223 reimbursement requests for care not needing authorisation (25 countries), 99.1% granted, EUR 118.15 million reimbursed. Germany reports most requests because it cannot separate Directive from Regulation cases. Flows are mostly between neighbours.
- European Commission (2025). *Trend report, reference years 2021 to 2023.* Published 22 September 2025. https://health.ec.europa.eu/publications/trend-report-reference-years-2021-2023-member-state-data-cross-border-patient-healthcare-following_en (summary page). 2021 to 2023 combined: 11,981 prior authorisation requests; 1,386,735 requests not subject to authorisation; Directive spending under 0.02% of total health expenditure of reporting countries. Do not compare the three-year total with the 2024 figure as a trend: country coverage differs by year, and Germany, which supplies most requests, cannot separate Directive cases from Regulation cases.
- Supports: Directive-based care is small and mostly between neighbours. Does not support: "few people get care abroad"; most cross-border care runs through the EHIC and S1/S2 channel in 4.1.

**4.3 People who live or move across borders (Eurostat and DG EMPL).**
- Eurostat, `migr_pop1ctz` (population on 1 January by citizenship), dataset updated 25 September 2026, extracted 2 October 2026, filters geo=EU27_2020, age=TOTAL, sex=T. Citizens of another EU27 Member State living in an EU country on 1 January 2025: **14,070,744** (3.1% of 450,646,971). Non-EU citizens: 30,600,204. This counts citizenship, not country of birth.
- Eurostat, `tour_dem_tttot` (trips by duration, purpose and destination), updated 18 September 2026, extracted 2 October 2026, geo=EU27_2020. Trips of 1+ nights by EU27 residents in 2024: **249,685,003 to other EU countries**, 342,657,389 abroad in total. Trips, not persons.
- De Wispelaere F, et al. (2026). *Posting of workers: report on A1 portable documents issued in 2024.* DG EMPL (full text via HIVA KU Leuven). 5.6 million PDs A1 issued in 2024; 3.6 million for posting (Article 12) to about 2.2 million persons.
- European Commission (2025). Erasmus+ news, 14 November 2025, https://erasmus-plus.ec.europa.eu/news/almost-15-million-people-went-on-an-erasmus-mobility-in-2024-according-to-latest-report : "Almost 1.5 million" students, learners and staff on Erasmus+ mobility in 2024 (no breakdown given).

**4.4 Beyond Europe.**

## Gaps worth stating on the page

- No study measures repeat tests, medication errors or adverse events caused by a record failing to cross a national border. The closest are the Commission's pharmacist survey (1.1.1), the public consultation (1.1.2) and Smith 2005's immigrant odds ratio (1.2.1).
- Nothing usable was found on migrant workers, students or athletes as patients whose records stay home. The population counts in 4.3 size the groups; they say nothing about harm.
- The HIE effect sizes are US and within-country. The EHDS impact assessment applies this kind of evidence to Europe by assumption.

## Ranked summary: the strongest usable figures

1. **EU care-side saving projected by the Commission:** about EUR 5.4 billion over 10 years, EU-wide, preferred option of the 2022 EHDS proposal; cross-border ePrescriptions and images are EUR 1.9 to 2.8 billion of that baseline, plus EUR 173 to 232 million from faster rollout (SWD(2022) 131). Projection, not measured.
2. **Unplanned care abroad under the EHIC:** about EUR 1.1 billion in 2024; 256 million EHICs (51% of citizens); 2.3 million people insured in one country and living in another, EUR 3.2 billion (De Wispelaere et al. 2026).
3. **14.07 million EU citizens living in another EU country on 1 January 2025; 249.7 million trips to other EU countries in 2024** (Eurostat).
4. **The record barely moves:** about 50,000 ePrescriptions and 18,000 dispensations exchanged through MyHealth@EU in 2025, and hundreds of patient summaries; 16 Member States plus Norway live (dashboard under revision); peer-reviewed snapshot in Bruthans and Jiráková 2023.
5. **Cross-border prescriptions fail at the counter:** about 7.8 million a year presented, 46% not dispensed, up to EUR 240 million a year (EHDS IA). Weak base: volume and rate both rest on 158 pharmacists in 5 countries, and the cost assumes a GP visit per failure.
6. **Records not sent home:** 41% of 181 consultation respondents said records went back home to a limited extent or not at all (SWD(2022) 200). Self-selected sample.
7. **Missing information, more often for recent immigrants:** missing in 13.6% of primary care visits, judged likely to harm in 44%; OR 1.78 for recent immigrants (Smith et al., *JAMA* 2005). US, 2003, clinician-reported.
8. **Emergency care without history:** information gaps in 32.2% of ED visits, adding 1.2 hours to the stay (Stiell et al., *CMAJ* 2003). Canada, single site.
9. **Exchange cuts repeat imaging:** HIE was associated with 8.7 points fewer repeat CTs (a 44% to 67% relative drop across modalities) in US EDs (Lammers et al. 2014); in the 2018 review all seven causal-design studies found benefit (Menachemi et al.), against an earlier null-leaning review (Rahurkar et al. 2015) and one counter-finding (McCormick et al. 2012). US and within-country.
10. **Medical travel comes home:** about 1.13 million US residents travelled abroad for planned care in 2016, and 67% sought follow-up care in the US (Stoney et al. 2022).
