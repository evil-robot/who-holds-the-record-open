# Deep literature review: will "Who Holds the Record" hold up under scholarly scrutiny?

**Purpose.** Test the claims in `paper/paper.md` (review draft, 2 October 2026) against the strongest published evidence in six areas: composite index method; patient access to records; consent models and the EHDS; public and private fragmentation (the strain-and-split layer); health data breaches; and the validity of agent-assisted evidence work. This extends `LITERATURE_REVIEW.md` (which looked for country-level coverage and prior art) and does not repeat it. Sources already in `references.bib` are cited by their existing key and not re-added.

Compiled 2 October 2026. Every source below was opened: abstract at minimum, full text where marked. Where only metadata could be read, the row says so. Nothing is cited from memory.

## Bottom line

1. **One sentence in the paper is contradicted by the record.** Section 6.1 says "We found no published index that scores countries on a person's rights over their own health record." The Health Consumer Powerhouse published the Euro Health Consumer Index (EHCI) from 2005 to at least 2018, scoring 35 European countries on 46 indicators in its 2018 edition (48 in 2016); indicator 1.5, "Access to own medical record", grades each country green, amber or red on whether patients can read their own records. HCP's *Empowerment of the European Patient 2009* scored 31 countries on 19 patient-empowerment indicators, 10 of them on patient rights. These are grey literature and are health-system or empowerment indices, not record-rights indices, but the sentence is false as written. Fix: "We found no index whose object is a person's rights over their own health record; the nearest, the Euro Health Consumer Index, scores record access as one of 46 health-system indicators [EHCI2018]."
2. **The method section will draw the standard composite-index critique.** The OECD/JRC Handbook (2008) treats uncertainty and sensitivity analysis as a required step (Step 7). The paper bootstraps its external correlations but never puts an interval on its own ranks, never tests other weights or aggregation, and admits a double-counted pair (r = 0.94) without correcting it. Saisana 2005, Paruolo 2013, Høyland 2012, Jacobs 2005 and Barclay 2019 all predict that a reviewer will ask for this. It is fixable with the repository's own 65 by 8 matrix.
3. **The agent method is defensible only if it is reported to evidence-synthesis norms.** The joint Cochrane, Campbell, JBI and CEE position statement (2025) says authors should report the AI system's name, version and dates, the justification, and how outputs were validated. The paper's "[OPEN: ... whether to name the agent platform and model]" is, under those norms, not optional. The legal-hallucination studies (Dahl 2024; Magesh 2025) show that tools citing real sources still misstate them 17% to 33% of the time, which is exactly the gap the paper itself concedes in Section 8.3 ("It does not check that the page says what the summary says"). Agent-checks-agent is weaker independence than it sounds (Kim 2025; Panickssery 2024).
4. **The substantive literature mostly supports the paper, with qualifications.** Patients who read their notes find errors (Bell 2020: 21% of readers reported a mistake, 42% of those serious), which supports Section 11. Opt-out raises consent and reduces bias versus opt-in (Hermus 2025 RCT), which sits awkwardly with a rubric that rewards an "individual" default. UK evidence links waiting lists to private insurance (Besley 1999); Australian evidence finds the link only for a high chance of a long wait (Johar 2011). Fragmentation harms are documented between hospitals inside one system (Snow 2020; Bourgeois 2010), so the public-private door is one split among several. Breach counts are a function of reporting rules (Liu 2015; Jiang 2025), which supports the paper's caveat on story counts and qualifies scoring "major breaches" in privacy.

## Definitions and method

- **Supports / qualifies / challenges.** Supports: the source's finding is consistent with the paper's sentence. Qualifies: consistent in direction but narrows scope, adds a condition, or shows a cost the paper does not name. Challenges: the source's finding, if accepted, makes the sentence false or the method below the field's standard.
- **Ranking.** By how likely a referee is to raise the point and how much of the paper it touches. Rank 1 to 4 are method and prior-art items a referee will raise first; substantive topics follow.
- **OA status.** From Unpaywall on 2 October 2026 (gold, hybrid, bronze, green, closed). Licence where Unpaywall reported one.
- **Search.** Europe PMC REST (sorted by citations), OpenAlex search, Crossref bibliographic query, Semantic Scholar, DataCite (for arXiv and OSF DOIs), Unpaywall, medRxiv API, NCBI eFetch and Europe PMC full-text XML; SerpAPI Google search for the grey literature. Queries are in Provenance.

## Claims at risk

Ordered by severity. Each quotes the paper.

1. **Prior art (contradicted).** "We found no published index that scores countries on a person's rights over their own health record." (6.1). EHCI 2005 to 2018 and HCP's 2009 empowerment index scored European countries on access to one's own record [EHCI2018, EHCI2016, HCP2009Empowerment]. Reword and cite; also adjust "Existing indices measure related things, mostly the digital maturity of health systems" (1).
2. **No uncertainty analysis on the ranks.** "Readers should treat differences under five points as ties." (3) and Table 2's full ranking. The Handbook's Step 7, Saisana 2005, Høyland 2012 and Barclay 2019 expect Monte Carlo rank intervals under perturbed weights, aggregation and score resolution. The bootstrap in Section 6.2 is on the external comparison only. Remedy: perturb weights (for example Dirichlet around the published weights), add plus or minus 5 noise per cell, try geometric aggregation, and report each country's rank interval and band stability.
3. **Weights and double counting.** "Access and control carry the most weight because they are the core of the question." (2.1) and "Together they carry 25% of the weight, so connected infrastructure is in effect counted twice." (7.4). Paruolo 2013 shows nominal weights rarely equal effective importance when inputs correlate; the Handbook says correct collinear pairs or justify them. Remedy: report each category's main effect on the overall score; either merge journey and clinician access or reweight, and show the ranking both ways.
4. **Full compensability, undiscussed.** "The overall score is their weighted average." (2.1). A weighted mean lets strong infrastructure offset weak consent or privacy [OECD2008]. One paragraph and a geometric-mean sensitivity run would answer it.
5. **AI reporting.** "[OPEN: authors to decide whether to name the agent platform and model.]" (3). Under the Cochrane, Campbell, JBI and CEE statement this must be named, with version, dates, purpose, validation and limitations [Flemyng2025]. Leaving it open falls below the standard those four organisations set for any evidence synthesis that uses AI.
6. **"Opening every source" as a guarantee.** "AI research agents did the research and scoring against a written rubric, opening every source they cite" (Abstract). Two problems. (a) Internal: the paper's own link check could not open 129 of 2,096 links (104 bot-blocked, 14 unreachable, 11 dead) (7.4), so "every" is a rule, not a verified fact; say "required to open". (b) External: retrieval does not prevent misstatement; legal tools that cite real sources still err 17% to 33% of the time [Magesh2025], and Section 8.3 concedes the claim-to-source check was not done. Remedy: a human audit of a random sample of cells (for example 10%, stratified by region and language) comparing each summary sentence with its source, with error rate and kappa reported.
7. **Independence of agent checks.** "a second agent then checked the research agents' work independently" (3); "the audit's cross-country reading was done by one agent" (7.4). Correlated errors across models [Kim2025] and self-preference [Panickssery2024] weaken "independently"; single-coder country indices fall below the multi-coder norm [Marquardt2018]. Remedy: state the models used for research and for checking; if the same family, drop "independently"; resolve the [OPEN] on a second reading of the original 43 with human or different-family coders on a sample.
8. **Verifier test size.** "We tested the verifier by planting eight rule violations; it caught all eight." (5.1). Eight of eight is consistent with a true catch rate as low as about 63% (exact two-sided 95% interval, lower bound 0.63; one-sided lower bound 0.69). Report the interval or plant more.
9. **Control default vs research representativeness.** "whether the default is individual or institutional control" (2.1) together with "A high score means the person's choice is respected and research is still possible." (2.1). Randomised evidence shows opt-in lowers consent and adds bias [Hermus2025; deMan2023]. The rubric should state how it scores opt-out with a working register against opt-in.
10. **"Works in practice" without equity.** "Leading (85 to 100): the right exists in law and works in practice at national scale, with controls the patient can see." (2.2). Portal offer and use differ by race, ethnicity and education [Richwine2023; Grossman2019], and opting out of My Health Record concentrated in particular groups [Holt2023]. Add equity as a named limitation.
11. **Breaches in the privacy score.** "major breaches" (2.1, privacy). Breach visibility tracks reporting law [Liu2015; Jiang2025]. Scoring major breaches risks rewarding opacity. State how cells handle countries without public breach registers.
12. **Strain and split measures.** "A public system short of doctors and nurses for the demand produces long waits. People who can pay turn to private care. Public and private providers keep different record systems, so the person's record breaks apart." (9.1). The waits-to-private link is supported in the UK [Besley1999] and only in the tail in Australia [Johar2011]; the paper's wait measure understates queues [Siciliani2026]; fragmentation harms occur between public hospitals too [Snow2020; Bourgeois2010]; and publicly funded private provision is a second route [Kirkwood2026]. Section 9.7 already says the causal step is untested, which is the right stance; cite these to show the links are plausible but conditional.
13. **Section 11 position.** "It is also not the same as the person holding the record." (11). Informatics consensus favours integrated over standalone records [Tang2006]; patient-held records are often incomplete [Joseph2021]. The section is labelled position, so this is a framing risk, not an error.

## Caveats from an adversarial read

1. **EHCI is grey literature from a private company.** It is not peer reviewed, its weights were changed after criticism, and its record-access data came partly from commissioned surveys and "personal interviews". Citing it as prior art does not endorse its method. The same ISBN is printed on the 2016 and 2018 reports; cite by title, year and URL. The last edition found is 2018 (published February 2019); whether it continued was not checked.
2. **EHDS commentary predates the adopted Regulation.** Marelli 2023, Staunton 2024, Shabani 2022 and van Kessel 2022 critique the May 2022 proposal. Use them as context for the direction of debate, not as analysis of Regulation 2025/327. [Martani2026] (already in the bib) addresses the adopted text.
3. **LLM evidence ages fast.** Most accuracy studies test 2023 to 2024 models (Claude 2, GPT-4). They neither prove nor disprove current agent accuracy; they show which checks the field expects. The only large positive study (otto-SR) is an unrefereed preprint.
4. **Agent studies are mostly English, biomedical and extraction-shaped.** None tests scoring a country's legal regime against a rubric across languages, which is the paper's task. The closest are the legal-hallucination studies (US case law) and Khraisha (multilingual, but screening and extraction).
5. **Breach evidence is US only.** HHS OCR is the only large public registry used in the literature found.
6. **Strain-and-split evidence is mostly from high-income systems** (UK, Australia, US, OECD). Basu 2012 is the only LMIC synthesis, and it says nothing about records.
7. **Metadata-only items.** McCoy 2018 and Jiang-Bai 2019 (research letters, full text 403) and Abouk 2024 (one-sentence summary) are included for completeness; do not quote figures from them. van Kessel 2022 and Shabani 2022: standfirst only. Høyland 2012 abstract from RePEc; Besley 1999 abstract from the working-paper version. Saltelli A (2007), "Composite indicators between analysis and advocacy", *Soc Indic Res* 81:65-77, doi:10.1007/s11205-006-0024-9: no abstract could be opened (Crossref, OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar, Springer page); not used and not added to the bib.
8. **Two OA statuses disagree with reality.** Unpaywall lists the OECD Handbook as closed; OECD serves the full PDF free. Treat the "closed" label there as wrong.
